Setting the scene

Quick Delivery, Inside the Biggest Sale of the Year

This case introduces no new concepts — every question is solved with something from the module. Amazon's Great Indian Festival 2026 started on October 8th, a day before Flipkart's Big Billion Days (October 9th). This year Amazon brought Amazon Now, its 10–15 minute delivery service, directly inside the main festival sale: shoppers can pick quick delivery right at search, without switching to Blinkit, Zepto or Swiggy Instamart.

Your role

Product Analyst, Amazon India

For the Great Indian Festival, Amazon has added Amazon Now (10–15 minute delivery) inside the main app. Shoppers can choose quick delivery right at search.

🔍 Search⚡ Choose quick delivery🛵 10–15 min
🧑‍💼
Leadership asks

"How will we know this is a success? What metrics would you track?"

Common first instincts

Where most first answers start

Asked this in a meeting or an interview, most people start in one of three places: jumping straight to a number ("number of orders"), asking what the goal is, or thinking about cost and user experience.

All three belong in a strong answer — but the order matters. The rest of this case shows that order, one step at a time.

Before answering, name the question type. Is this a metric change question or a metric definition question?
Answer: B — a metric definition question. Nothing has moved yet; leadership wants to know what to measure. So we use the 4-step framework from Product Strategy and Product Metrics I: Clarify → Business Goals → Define Metrics → Summarize.

Checkpoint 1

Why Is Amazon Doing This?

Clarify starts with the main objective behind the release. A metric without a strategy behind it is just a number.

1
Framework step · Clarify — the objective (Product Strategy)
Find the strategy behind the feature
Why would Amazon put 10–15 minute delivery inside its biggest sale of the year?
💡Where do users go today when they need something in 10–15 minutes? Who is Amazon losing those orders to?
Answer: A — compete with quick-commerce apps for urgent purchases.

Quick-commerce apps are pulling urgent and impulse orders away from traditional e-commerce. With quick delivery inside the main app, the shopper doesn't need to switch apps when they need something fast. It's a defensive and a growth move at the same time.

Why the others fail
  • BInventory clearance is driven by discounts, not delivery speed — and quick delivery only covers items stocked in local centres.
  • CDownloads are a vanity outcome (Product Metrics I). Most festival shoppers already have the Amazon app.
  • D10–15 minute delivery needs local centres and dedicated riders, so cost per order usually goes up, not down.
🎯 Keep this objective in mind. Every metric picked in the rest of the case must connect back to it.
Checkpoint 2

How Big Is the Prize?

Before leadership cares about metrics, they ask a simpler question: is this even worth it? Nobody will hand you the number. Estimate it the guesstimate way: Clarify → Analyze → Breakdown → Calculate → Validate.

2
Framework step · Guesstimate
How many quick-delivery orders on a festival day?
What is the best starting point to break down daily Amazon Now orders during the sale?
💡Start from the group of people who can actually use the feature. Who can order with 10–15 minute delivery today?
Answer: D — daily active shoppers in Amazon Now cities.

Only shoppers in serviceable cities can use the feature, so starting there keeps every later assumption grounded. It's the same narrowing-funnel pattern as the iPhone guesstimate (population → eligible → can afford → market share), just from a tighter base.

Why the others fail
  • AThe whole population adds several shaky layers before you even reach Amazon shoppers.
  • BA competitor's numbers describe a different user base and business.
  • CApp installs aren't linked to festival shopping in Amazon Now cities.

The last step is Validate: does the number make sense? One way to check it independently:

Check it from the supply side: if Amazon runs about 800 local centres, each handling about 400 orders a day at festival peak, they can deliver about 3.2 lakh orders a day. Hold that thought: of these 3 lakh orders, are all of them new for Amazon? Many would have been ordered anyway with standard delivery. That question decides the business goal in Checkpoint 4.

Checkpoint 3

Clarify the Feature

Interviewers notice when a candidate jumps straight to metrics without making sure everyone means the same thing. In the Facebook 'Save' case, we clarified what the feature does and who benefits. Same here.

3
Framework step · Clarify — what, problem, who benefits
One line: what problem does it solve, and for whom?

State it in one line: what problem it solves and who benefits.

💡Template: "It helps [who] to [do what], so that [problem solved]." Then ask: who else besides shoppers gains from it?

Too vague

"Fast delivery for everyone." — No user, no problem, no reason it matters.

Incomplete

Names only the shopper — and misses the seller and the business.

The ideal answer

"It helps festival shoppers who need items urgently get them in 10–15 minutes inside the main Amazon app, so they don't switch to another quick-commerce app."

🛍️ Shoppers

Get urgent items fast without leaving Amazon.

🏪 Sellers

Sell more of their fast-moving items during the sale.

🏢 Amazon

Keeps urgent orders that would otherwise go to a competitor.

🔑 Notice the word "urgently". Urgent orders are the ones Amazon would otherwise lose to a quick-commerce app — and they shape the business goal next.
Checkpoint 4

Set the Business Goals

Step 2 states the goal for each stakeholder, because success is measured against these goals. Shopper and seller goals are easy. The business goal is where most candidates make their first mistake.

4
Framework step · Business Goals
Two of these options look very similar
What should be Amazon's business goal for adding Amazon Now inside the festival sale?
💡If a user would have bought the same item anyway with standard delivery, did Amazon actually gain anything from that quick-delivery order?
Answer: C — genuinely new orders per active user.

Remember the Indian Bank case in Product Metrics II: before calling app revenue a success, we separated genuinely new revenue from revenue that merely shifted channels. Same here. If a user switches an order from standard to quick delivery, the quick-delivery count goes up, but Amazon hasn't won anything new — and has probably paid more to deliver it. The genuinely new orders are called incremental orders.

Why the others fail
  • ACounts orders that would have happened anyway. The most common trap — Checkpoint 8 comes back to it.
  • BTraffic is an awareness signal, not an outcome. A sale drives traffic on its own.
  • DCity expansion is an operational input. It helps reach, but doesn't show whether the feature creates value.

Business goals for each stakeholder

Shopper goal

Get urgent items in 10–15 minutes without switching apps.

Seller goal

Sell more fast-moving items during the sale.

Business goal

Increase incremental orders per active user, delivered at a sustainable cost.

💰 The words "sustainable cost" matter. We'll need guardrail metrics that protect them.
Checkpoint 5

Define Metrics: Walk Through AAAERR

Just like the Facebook 'Save' case, walk the user through each stage and define the metric that shows success there. Not every stage applies to every feature.

5
Framework step · Define Metrics — AAAERR
Pick a stage, name a metric

Define one metric for each stage, following the shopper through the journey.

💡Follow the shopper: they search, see the quick-delivery option, choose it, receive the order, and maybe come back. Which stage is each of those moments?

AAAERR for Amazon Now

Awareness

% of shoppers who saw the quick-delivery option at least once during the sale

Acquisition

Mostly not applicable — festival shoppers are already Amazon customers. At most: new customers whose first Amazon order was a quick-delivery order.

Activation

Quick-delivery selection rate = shoppers who chose quick delivery ÷ shoppers shown the option

Engagement

% of quick-delivery orders delivered within the 10–15 minute promise; quick-delivery orders per active user

Revenue

Incremental orders per active user; average order value (AOV) of quick-delivery orders

Retention

30-day repeat quick-delivery rate — shoppers who order with quick delivery again within 30 days

Each stage answers a different question: did shoppers notice it, try it, did it work for them, did it make money, did they come back? A drop at any stage tells you exactly where the feature is leaking.

👀 The stage most people skip is Awareness — the same one AI often leaves thin, as we saw in Lecture 1. And during a festival rush, the stage most likely to break is Engagement: items not in stock locally, or late deliveries.

Judge the AI's Metric List

In Lecture 1, we asked AI to define success metrics for 'Save for Later' using AAAERR and then checked its work. Same here: AI gives a fast first draft; the analyst's job is to judge it.

"Act as a product analyst. Amazon India has added Amazon Now (10–15 minute delivery) inside its main app during the Great Indian Festival sale. List 8 metrics that would define success, structured using AAAERR: Awareness, Acquisition, Activation, Engagement, Revenue, Retention."

Which of these metrics is a vanity metric?
💡From Product Metrics I: a vanity metric looks good on a dashboard but doesn't help you make a decision. Which number can go up even if nobody orders anything?
Answer: B — banner impressions.

Impressions rise just because the sale drives traffic. They say nothing about whether shoppers chose quick delivery or whether Amazon gained an order. App downloads (#2) are vanity for the same reason. For Awareness, use the actionable version from Checkpoint 5: % of shoppers who saw the option.

Why the others fail
  • AActionable — as long as it's read alongside total orders per active user (see Checkpoint 8).
  • CActionable — tells operations exactly where the promise is breaking.
  • DActionable — tells us whether the feature is becoming a habit.
What's missing?

Every metric on the AI's list measures success. What kind of metric is missing?

💡Go back to the business goal: incremental orders, at a sustainable cost. What must not get worse because of this feature?

🛡️ Delivery cost per order

Quick delivery is expensive — growth that loses money isn't success.

🛡️ Cancellation & refund rate

Items shown as available but out of stock at the local centre.

🛡️ Total orders per active user

Cannibalization check — are quick-delivery orders new, or just moved from standard delivery?

These are guardrail metrics. In Product Strategy, a feature could win on its own scoreboard while quietly hurting the product around it — like Reels pulling time away from the main feed. Guardrails catch that. The AI's list has none.

🤖 The AI produced a decent, well-structured first draft in seconds — but included two vanity metrics and missed every guardrail. AI drafts, the analyst decides.
Checkpoint 7

Label the Metrics, Pick the One to Act On

Once the metrics are listed, Product Metrics I says to label them: one North Star, then Primary (L1) and Supporting (L2).

⭐ North Star
Incremental orders per active user
Primary (L1)
Quick-delivery selection rate · 30-day repeat quick-delivery rate
Supporting (L2)
% delivered within the promise · quick-delivery orders per active user · AOV of quick-delivery orders · selection rate by city
🛡️ Guardrails
Delivery cost per order · cancellation & refund rate · total orders per active user
🗑️ Vanity (drop)
Banner impressions · App downloads
7
Framework step · Define Metrics — North Star, L1, L2
The sale lasts ~10 days. One metric goes on the daily war-room dashboard.
Which metric should go on the daily war-room dashboard during the sale?
💡Product Metrics I said an L1 metric can be more useful than the North Star in the short term. Why? Think about how quickly each metric gives you feedback.
Answer: D — quick-delivery selection rate.

An L1 metric has a narrower focus and a tighter feedback loop than the North Star. Selection rate updates every day from existing data, and the team can act on it the same day — fix stock, visibility or placement. The North Star says whether we're winning overall, but it can only be read properly from an experiment (Checkpoint 9).

Why the others fail
  • AA vanity metric, as Checkpoint 6 showed.
  • BA good L1, but it takes 30 days to measure — longer than the sale. Great for the post-sale review, not a daily decision.
  • CThe right measure of overall success, but incremental orders need a comparison group without the feature, so there's no same-day feedback.
⚠️ Selection rate is for acting every day, not for judging success. It can rise while the business gains nothing — which is why it always sits next to a guardrail.
Checkpoint 8

The 50% Trap

Time to test the metrics against a real situation. The first week of the sale has passed, and your manager walks up with a dashboard and a big smile.

8
Framework step · Define Metrics — guardrails (cannibalization)
"Quick-delivery orders are up 50%!"
🧑‍💼

"In the first week of the sale, quick-delivery orders are up 50% against our pre-sale forecast! Can we call this a success?"

What's your answer to the manager?
💡Remember the business goal from Checkpoint 4. Does a 50% rise in quick-delivery orders tell you anything about genuinely new orders?
Answer: A — check total orders per active user first.

The full dashboard (below) shows the 1 lakh extra quick-delivery orders are exactly the 1 lakh orders standard delivery lost. Total orders haven't moved — shoppers simply switched delivery options. This is cannibalization, the C in TROPiCS. And because quick delivery costs more per order, Amazon may be earning less on the same orders.

Why the others fail
  • BCelebrates a metric that only measures movement between delivery options, not new value. This is why we check before we celebrate.
  • CCost may be a concern, but you can't conclude it without cost data. The bigger issue is that total orders are flat.
  • DA steady rise can still be pure switching. Day-by-day consistency says nothing about whether total orders grew.

Checkpoints 9 & 10

Prove It Works, Then Decide Where to Launch

Comparing raw numbers didn't work. Leadership now asks: "Fine — how do we prove whether Amazon Now creates genuinely new orders?" This is where A/B testing comes in.

9
Framework step · A/B Testing
How do we prove it works?
What's the best way to prove Amazon Now drives incremental orders?
💡For each option, ask: is there anything other than Amazon Now that could explain the difference between the two groups?
Answer: B — a randomized treatment vs control split.

Random assignment makes the two groups alike in every way except the feature, so any real difference in orders per active user can be credited to Amazon Now. That difference is the North Star: incremental orders per active user.

Why the others fail
  • AFestival week always beats a normal week. Seasonality (the External Data step of CRIED) drives the difference, not the feature.
  • CSample bias. Shoppers who choose quick delivery are already urgent, high-intent shoppers — they'd order more anyway.
  • DToo much changed in a year: prices, competitors, city coverage, user base.

Designing the experiment

Hypotheses

H₀: Amazon Now makes no difference to orders per active user.
H₁: it changes orders per active user.

Sample size

Set from the baseline, the minimum detectable effect leadership cares about (3% lift), a 5% significance level and 80% power. A tool does the calculation.

Duration

Sample size ÷ eligible shoppers per day — and run through the full sale plus 2–3 weeks after, so a novelty effect doesn't inflate the result.

Metrics

Success: orders per active user. Guardrails: delivery cost per order, cancellation rate.

🧪 Why can't we just compare shoppers who used quick delivery with those who didn't? Sample bias — they chose it because they were already more likely to buy.
10
Framework step · A/B Testing & Launch Recommendations
Read the results: launch or not?

Decision rule agreed with leadership: scale Amazon Now in the main app if orders per active user rise by at least 3% and the lift is statistically significant (p < 0.05), delivery cost per order stays within budget, and cancellation rate doesn't rise by more than 1 percentage point.

What is your launch recommendation?
💡The A/B Testing lecture asked what to do when results conflict: the success metric goes up, but a guardrail goes the wrong way. Check every condition of the decision rule, city type by city type.
Answer: D — ship in metro, fix tier-2 costs first.

Metro passes every condition: +3.8% (significant, above 3%), cost within budget, cancellations +0.3 pp. Tier-2 passes on orders and cancellations but fails the cost guardrail. That's the conflicting-results case from the lecture: ship where everything passes, hold where a guardrail fails, and improve tier-2 costs (for example, by batching deliveries).

Why the others fail
  • AIgnores a guardrail breach. Guardrails exist to stop good-looking launches that lose money.
  • BThrows away a proven, significant gain in metro cities.
  • CResults are already significant in both city types. Six more months delays value without adding much.

Link it to business impact: on about 12 lakh orders a day, a 4% lift is roughly 48,000 extra orders a day. That's the number leadership will remember. And remember: a launch recommendation is rarely just yes or no — the strongest answers say where to ship, where to hold, and what must change first.

Wrap-up

From First Instinct to a Structured Answer

A first instinct often jumps straight to a single number. The stronger approach is: clarify the objective and who benefits, set the business goals, walk through AAAERR with guardrails, label the North Star, and summarize with a recommendation. And "maximise quick-delivery orders" — the most tempting goal in Checkpoint 4 — is exactly the metric Checkpoint 8 showed would have fooled us.

Key Takeaways

1
Identify the question type first

A metric definition question uses Clarify → Business Goals → Define Metrics → Summarize.

2
Start from the objective, then size the prize

Every metric must connect to why the business built the feature. A quick guesstimate, validated against an independent number, tells leadership whether it's worth the effort.

3
Separate new value from shifted value

Watch for cannibalization whenever a new option competes with an existing one.

4
Always add guardrails

Growth that loses money or breaks the customer promise is not success.

5
Label your metrics

One North Star; L1 metrics for fast, daily action; L2 metrics for detail; drop the vanity ones.

6
AI drafts, the analyst decides

Use AI for speed, then remove vanity metrics and add the guardrails it misses.

7
Prove impact with an A/B test, and ship where every guardrail passes

Random assignment beats every before/after or user-vs-non-user comparison.

8
Use RFM to decide who to retain first

High-value shoppers going quiet come before the biggest or the most active groups.
